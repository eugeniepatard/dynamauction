A game about planning

The Knight's Merchant

Buy the gear a knight ordered, one market stand at a time. Each purchase changes what the next stands are worth to you: the bargain you take now can be the better one you miss later.

The story

You are a travelling merchant. A knight has ordered five pieces of gear and written on your parchment what he will pay for each one.

Today you walk through the market, stand after stand, to buy them as cheaply as you can before you reach his castle.

The rules

  1. The market has 20 stands, visited in order. You cannot go back.
  2. Each stand sells one piece of your order at a posted price: buy it, or walk on.
  3. You need each piece once. When you own it, no later stand offers it: they sell only what is still missing.
  4. A piece's price is anywhere between 30% and 120% of what the knight pays, all prices equally likely. You don't know what the next stand sells, nor at what price.
  5. The day ends when your order is complete or after the 20th stand.

Your score

profit = Σ over the pieces you bought (knight's price − price paid)

A missing piece earns nothing. Buy the sword (the knight pays 55) for 42: +13. Pass, and if no cheaper sword comes before the castle, the sword earns 0.

A price below the knight's value is a sure profit now, but a cheaper one may come, and fewer stands remain to find the rest.

    What does this have to do with DynamAuction?

    The game is a small version of the problem DynamAuction is built for: a buyer meets a stream of opportunities, and every purchase changes what the next ones are worth.

    Dynamic value

    Once you own a sword, the next sword is worth nothing to you, and the missing pieces show up more often. In DynamAuction the same thing happens to an advertiser: each ad a user sees changes the user's state (here, the pieces you own; there, the user's fatigue), and so the value of the next displays.

    A posted price is a second-price auction seen from the buyer's side: the price is the highest rival bid, and buying means bidding above it.

    Myopic or planner?

    The myopic merchant buys whenever the price is below what the knight pays: a sure profit now, blind to what the next stands may bring. The planner compares each price with what it can still expect from the stands left (computed exactly, by dynamic programming): picky at first, less and less as the castle gets closer.

    In the paper, the same gap separates the myopic bidders from our MPC planner. See the results.
